All honest men are dead (Bohemian Rhapsody short story)

The country is Bohemia, Central Europe. You might have heard about it from one of the most popular Sherlock Holmes stories, A Scandal in Bohemia. The story itself takes place in England, but the infamous Irene Adler managed to blackmail our king. Which, frankly, is something of a tradition for our monarchs.

The second popular thing is Bohemian Rhapsody, written when Freddie and the boys lived in our country for a while. At the time, the current king's predecessor proposed that Freddie become his Queen. The constitution was rewritten overnight, making Bohemia the first country in Europe to legalise gay marriage. Sadly, the neighbouring countries didn't recognise this particular legal achievement, and Freddie eventually changed his mind and left.

After that, Bohemia became a popular immigration destination for gays, lesbians, and other queer folk. The result is that about forty-five percent of our population is not straight.

But that’s not important.

The story I'm about to tell took place during the COVID pandemic, back when everyone was wearing masks, washing their hands, and developing strong opinions about personal distance. Lotty was not particularly famous at the time. He had a few clients, and I – as his poor sidekick – had much more free time. His fame would come later, after the unfortunate business with the giant snake.

You all know THAT story, so I won’t be repeating myself. And if you have no idea who Lotty is… don’t worry, you will find out very soon.

I’d rather start this story with a young woman walking into our office.

I was sitting at my desk, attempting to convince a printer that it had no reason to hate me personally, when she knocked on the open door. She was tall, broad-shouldered, and wearing a medical mask, which was hardly unusual at the time. She looked somewhere in her early twenties, with light hair tied back in a ponytail and the slightly exhausted expression in her eyes.

"Are you a private detective?" she asked. 

"Depends on who's asking," I replied.

She hesitated.

"Professor Richards, from the Queen’s medical college recommended you. He said you might be able to help me."

"Then you're in luck," I said. "The private detective is over here."

I pointed towards Lotty's desk. He was sitting in his favourite chair, scrolling through his phone, with a half-eaten packet of crisps beside him. I could tell from the expression on his face that he had no intention of helping me with the printer.

"Come in, darling!" he called, as if seeing her only just now. "Don't stand in the doorway. We're not charging for the air in here."

The young woman entered and sat down.

"My name is Carol Adorah," she said. "I'm a student at the Queen’s medical college."

"Wonderful,” nodded Lotty. “I love doctors. They always tell me I'm perfectly healthy, and then I ignore everything they say."

"That’s your college," I pointed out, “which you’ve graduated from.”

Because yes, before becoming a pain in my ass and Bohemia's darling detective, Lotty once was a doctor. Nothing good came from it, of course.

"Do not tell lies to the young ladies,” said Lotty, immediately following with another lie, “me and professor Richards are just very good pals.”

Carol nodded politely, although I wasn't sure what her real expression was under the mask.

"So, what brings you here?" Lotty asked.

Carol folded her hands in her lap.

"I think something might be wrong at the house where I work. I… work part-time as a housekeeper, you see."

"A housekeeper?"

"Yes, for an elderly gentleman. It is not an especially pleasant job, but if I want to be a doctor in the future, I can start preparing to deal with all kinds of people, right?"

“Absolutely right, sweetheart,” said Lotty approvingly. See, if you don’t know, Lotty can be really performatively gay at times. Don’t get me wrong, Lotty is the gayest gay when no one is looking too, but he really enjoys the spectacle that comes with it. Sometimes he gets a lot of hate, from both gays and straights, but sometimes it is useful. Like now, when he somehow sensed that being obnoxious will help the pure woman relax. 

"Mr Edmond, my employee, he is not really nice, but not a bad person either. Just a bitter old gentleman who got hurt by life. Or so I thought."

Lotty leaned back in his chair.

"Go on. Tell us what made you change your mind."

Carol explained that she had been working for Edmond for several months. The salary was very good, considering that her duties were relatively simple. She cleaned the apartment, prepared meals, did the laundry, and occasionally bought groceries.

"He doesn't like people very much," she admitted. "Stays at home most of the time. Hates politics.”

"Ah. A man after my own heart."

I looked at Lotty. He was currently dating someone totally opposite, which made his claim somewhat questionable.

Carol continued: "There is a room in his apartment that I'm not allowed to enter. It's always locked. He told me very clearly that I shouldn't go inside, and I haven't."

"Oh, the classic bluebeard problem?" said Lotty. “Did you smell blood? Seen other women enter the room and never come out again?”

She looked at him as if he was crazy.

"Of course not! I would have called the police immediately. But sometimes he goes in there by himself."

"Well, it's his apartment," I said.

"Yes, but sometimes I hear him talking."

"On the telephone?"

"He doesn't have a landline, and his mobile phone is always on his desk."

"Perhaps he talks to himself."

"Perhaps," Carol agreed. "But it sounds like he's talking to somebody else. And he never talks to himself in other places."

Lotty stopped scrolling through his phone.

"How often does this happen?"

"Not every day. Maybe once or twice a week. Sometimes he stays in there for twenty minutes, sometimes longer."

"Have you ever heard another voice?"

"No. Only his."

"Then perhaps he's rehearsing conversations."

"With whom?"

"His enemies. His former wives. The tax authorities. I find that talking to people who aren't present is much more convenient than talking to people who are."

"Lotty," I said.

"What? I'm making a perfectly reasonable suggestion."

Carol looked even more worried.

"There are other things, too. He sometimes comes out looking upset. Once I found him standing in the corridor, staring at the door. He looked so sad. And he told me so many times not to enter there. "

"Had you?"

"No, of course not."

"Perhaps it’s someone’s ashes. A sacred altar not to be disturbed by anyone."

"I don't think so. Why wouldn’t he just say so? What is the point of secrecy? I am only happy to clean one room less. And there is another thing – he doesn’t let anyone inside the apartment. He doesn’t have visitors, has no friends."

"Well, he lets you inside the apartment," I pointed out.

"That's because I'm a woman."

"That's an interesting policy."

Carol nodded.

"He's had several male housekeepers before me. None of them lasted very long. He seems to think men are dishonest. He once told me that you couldn't trust a man to do anything unless he was being watched."

"That's a rather sweeping generalisation," I said.

"Perhaps he has had some unpleasant experiences," Lotty suggested. “I can totally relate.”

"Maybe. But he also checks the windows every evening, makes me lock the front door twice, and has a panic button in his room. He doesn't even like me bringing my own cleaning products because he thinks someone might have tampered with them."

"Does he have any family?" Lotty asked.

"Not that I know of. He never talks about them."

"And you're worried that he might be keeping something or someone in that room?"

Carol nodded.

"I know it sounds ridiculous. But what if there is somebody in there? What if he's holding someone against their will? I can't just leave without finding out."

I was about to point out how improbable this is, considering all the things, but Lotty raised a hand.

"Well, I've got an idea."

I turned towards him. This was unusual. Lotty generally needed at least three cups of coffee, a manicure, and a considerable amount of encouragement before coming up with an idea that involved actual work.

"I'll go there myself," he announced.

Carol blinked. I almost choked.

"Excuse me?"

"I'll pretend to be you. I'll enter the apartment, wait until your employer is distracted, and have a look inside that room. Simple."

I stared at him.

"You're going to do what?"

"Impersonate Carol."

"Yes, I heard you. I'm just hoping that if I ask again, you'll come up with a different answer."

Lotty ignored me.

"Think about it. You're tall, aren't you, darling?"

Carol nodded.

"Average weight?"

"More or less."

"Similar hair colour to mine?"

She looked at him, then at me.

"Yes, I suppose."

"And you always wear a mask?"

"We are in the midst of an epidemic here. Everyone should…"

"Excellent. We have practically everything in common."

"Except that she's a young woman," I said.

"Details."

"And Edmond knows her."

"Does he talk to her? Carol, dear, does he talk to you often?"

"Not very much, but…"

"Perfect! We barely need to act."

I looked at Lotty again.

I had worked with him for years, and although he occasionally demonstrated an impressive ability to understand people, he was not Sherlock Holmes. He was not particularly good at disguises, either. In fact, I couldn't remember him ever successfully disguising himself as anyone.

Other famous detectives seemed to manage it effortlessly. Sherlock Holmes could transform himself into practically anyone. Poirot pretended to be his own brother, which was an achievement considering that he didn't have one. Even Nero Wolfe once left his orchids behind to do some actual detective work.

Lotty, on the other hand, had once tried to wear sunglasses indoors and been recognised by a person who had never met him before.

"Are you sure?" Carol asked cautiously.

"Absolutely."

"Could you perhaps show us what you have in mind?" I asked.

Lotty smiled happily.

"Of course."

He stood up, disappeared behind the door to his private room, and returned a few minutes later.

I closed my eyes.

I really should have stayed home that day.

Lotty was wearing a skirt, a blouse, and a rather unfortunate ginger wig. He had applied some mascara and glitter, his attempt at makeup making him look like a person who had recently kissed a drug queen.

He was also wearing a medical mask, which didn’t help at all. I immediately took two dozen photos, but they all were destroyed later. Unfortunately for everyone involved, the scene was forever saved in my memory. 

"Well?" Lotty asked, striking a pose.

Carol stared at him.

I stared at him.

The printer behind me finally started working, apparently deciding that whatever was happening in the office was more interesting than our previous disagreement.

"You look..." Carol began.

"Beautiful?" Lotty suggested.

"Different," I said.

"Different from what?"

"From a woman."

Perhaps the quarantine had finally driven him insane, I thought. It wasn't as if he had been particularly sane before COVID. He spent most of his time at home anyway, and the only people he regularly saw were his boyfriends, his manicure specialist, and me when he needed something.

Maybe this was what happened when you locked a detective indoors for too long.

Carol cleared her throat.

"Actually, I think there's a much easier solution."

Lotty tilted his head, I bet thinking it’s a very cute little pose. It looked horrendous.

"I could simply let you into the apartment when Edmond isn't home,” Carol said. “You wouldn't need to pretend to be me."

There was a brief silence, then Lotty smiled.

"Well, darling, that would certainly work."

I looked at him.

He looked at me.

And I realised that he had probably waited for this all along. I sometimes wonder whether Lotty's greatest talent is solving mysteries or making other people do things against their will.

On that particular day, I was leaning towards the second.

Meanwhile Lotty sailed back to his chair, as if nothing was amiss. 

"How exactly did you find this job, darling?" he asked Carol.

"I saw an advertisement in the newspaper," she explained. "It was looking for a housekeeper, preferably female who could work flexible hours. My boyfriend recommended it to me."

"You have a boyfriend?" Lotty asked eagerly. “What about you show me yours, I’ll show you mine?”

Carol laughed.

"Well, I don’t anymore. We dated for a few months, but it didn't really work out with all the jobs and studies. We're still friends, though. Sort of. I suppose he's more of a hookup now."

"How considerate," Lotty said approvingly. "I love people who can maintain a healthy relationship after a breakup. Unlike some of my exes, who seem to think that being dumped is a personal attack on their entire existence."

I decided not to remind him that he had once sent a three-page message to an ex-boyfriend because the man had forgotten to return a borrowed cookbook.

"Do you have a photograph?" Lotty asked.

Carol looked at him.

"Of Edmond?"

"Of your hookup, darling. I'm not particularly interested in Edmond's appearance. I imagine he's old and angry, and that's quite enough information for me."

"Oh. Sure."

She took out her phone, opened Tinder, and scrolled through her matches.

Lotty leaned forward.

Now, Lotty was never a subtle person. He could pretend to be interested in someone's problems while secretly thinking about lunch, but when something genuinely caught his attention, his entire face changed. He stopped looking like a badly damaged drag queen, and started looking like a village aunty who had discovered an interesting piece of gossip.

"That's him," Carol said, turning the screen towards us.

The photograph showed a fair-haired man in his late twenties, with a neatly trimmed beard and a rather pleasant smile. He was wearing a black shirt and standing in front of what looked like an expensive restaurant.

"Handsome," Lotty observed.

"He's not bad," Carol agreed.

"What's his name? His job?"

"Jake. I think he does something with stocks, but I never asked for the details."

"And how long did you date?"

"About three months."

"Why did you break up?"

Carol shrugged.

"We wanted different things. He was nice enough, but I don't think he was particularly serious about me. And I was busy with university. We still meet sometimes, but it's not really a relationship."

"Does he know about that closed room problem?"

"Yes. He's the one who recommended that I apply for the job, so I told him. He said it’s probably something small, but that I should resign if I feel uncomfortable. The thing is… I think I don’t want to resign just yet. I want to know if Mr Edmond is really a horrible person."

Lotty looked at the Tinder profile again.

"It says your Jake is bi. Maybe I should try and hook up with him too, while we are at it."

I rolled my eyes.

"Lotty."

"Nothing, darling. I'm merely appreciating the wonders of modern technology."

He handed the phone back to Carol, while I was beginning to suspect that this case might be more complicated, despite all the nonsense.

"But you said… you have a boyfriend, no?" asked Carol hesitatingly. 

"Well, you don’t know it dear, but he's in Greece now. And he has COVID. How stupid it is? Apparently, the airline won't let him fly until he's recovered and tested negative."

"That doesn't mean you've broken up."

"No, but it does mean that I haven't seen him for ages. And, frankly, I'm beginning to think we should downgrade our relationship to something more manageable."

"Such as?"

"Hookup."

Carol laughed. She seemed to be enjoying herself, which was more than I could say for myself.

"Don't encourage him," I said. "He thinks he is funny, but he really is not."

"Anyway, darling," Lotty ignored me, talking back to Carol, "you mentioned that you're studying medicine. What year are you in?"

"Third."

"Do you enjoy it?"

"Most of the time. I like the practical classes, but it’s such a pain to memorize all the names."

"Understandable. When I opened a textbook called ‘Receptors in pharmacology’ I immediately wanted to kill someone too, so I’ve decided to solve crimes instead."

Carol smiled.

"I'd rather be a doctor than a detective, though."

"Good choice. Doctors have a much better reputation."

"And they get paid more," I added.

"Not always," Carol said. "At least not while they're studying. That's why I need the housekeeping job. My parents help me when they can, but I don't want to keep asking them for money."

She spoke quite matter-of-factly, without trying to make her circumstances sound worse than they were. I liked that.

"Well," Lotty said, "let's make sure your employer isn't keeping a secret prisoner in his spare room. We wouldn't want you to have to find another job."

***

We arranged for Carol to contact us when Edmond was expected to be away for a few hours.

It took another week before everything was organised.

By then, Nikos had returned from Greece, looking considerably healthier than the last time I had seen him. He had also brought Lotty a rather expensive box of chocolates, which suggested that their relationship is not being downgraded anytime soon.

Maybe the whole thing was simply an excuse to complain.

Nikos was a useful addition to our little case, though. He was tall, athletic, and much more discreet than Lotty. We asked him to keep an eye on Edmond and make sure the old man didn't return while we were inside the apartment.

"Why me?" he asked.

"Because I’m too handsome," Lotty replied.

"You are, but why?"

"Why? It explains everything, darling. People notice me. They notice you too, but in a different way."

I had stopped trying to understand their relationship some time ago.

***

Carol met us outside Edmond's apartment building. She looked nervous, although she had made an effort to appear calm.

"He's gone shopping," she explained. "It usually takes about an hour."

"Excellent," Lotty said. "Let's not waste his absence."

She unlocked the door and led us inside.

Edmond's apartment was tidy and sad. There were no family photographs on the walls. No pictures of friends, no souvenirs from holidays, no obvious signs that anyone else had ever lived there.

"Does he always keep it like this?" I whispered.

"Yes," Carol replied. "He doesn't like clutter."

"Neither do I," Lotty said. "But I do like having people over. It makes the clutter less noticeable."

Carol took us down the corridor and stopped in front of a closed door.

"This is it."

Lotty examined the lock.

"Does he keep the key?"

"Yes. He carries it with him."

"Good thinking."

Lotty opened his purse and took out a small picklock. Which, I should stress it for my readers, is absolutely not legal to possess or use to enter locked rooms. In this case I write about it freely, as all involved parties are either unable or unwilling to press charges. Let us all collectively imagine that it was the only time in his life when Lotty used the instrument. 

We entered the room.

I was expecting something sinister at this point. I know Lotty very well, and he would never lift a finger without a reason. Perhaps there was really a person held captive, I thought—in a dark cage with soundproofed walls. Or at least a mysterious submarine layouts in a locked cabinet, or a collection of stolen knives that would make me regret having come along.

Instead,we found an ordinary office.

There was a desk, a chair, a bookshelf, and a tall fig palm in a pot. The room was a bit dusty but nothing particularly unusual about it.

"Well," I said.

"Yes," Lotty replied.

"That's disappointing."

"Don't be so impatient. We haven't looked properly yet."

He walked around the room, examining the furniture and the walls. I checked the bookshelf, which contained a few old books and some folders. There were no photographs, no documents that seemed particularly interesting, and certainly no evidence of anyone being held against their will.

Carol stood near the door, looking increasingly uncomfortable.

"Do you think he just comes here to be alone?" she asked.

"Possibly," Lotty said. Then he stopped, looking somewhere above her left shoulder.

"What is it?" I asked, alarmed by his face. Which suddenly seemed very green.

He was looking upwards, towards one corner of the room.

I followed his gaze and found a small security camera mounted near the ceiling.

"Oh shit," Lotty murmured. “How paranoid is he, exactly?”

I was about to suggest that we should leave as soon as possible when I heard a key turning in the front door.

Carol went green too.

"He's back."

Lotty’s phone buzzed, as Nikos most probably was trying to tell us that Edmond is heading back. That’s the problem with following someone. I have no idea how Sherlock Holmes did it, in Lotty’s case it almost never worked. 

There was no time to do anything sensible.

The footsteps approached along the corridor, and a moment later Edmond appeared in the doorway.

He was a thin elderly man, with grey hair, a sharply defined nose hidden behind the medical mask, and a very disappointed expression.

He looked at Carol.

Then at me.

Then at Lotty.

"What," he finally demanded, "are you doing in my house?"

Carol opened her mouth.

"I can explain—"

"Explain?" he repeated. "You have brought strangers into my apartment! Into my private room!"

"I'm sorry, Mr Edmond. I only—"

"You're fired!" He turned towards her, his face reddening. "I told you never to enter this room. I told you explicitly. And now you've brought these people here? How dare you?"

"Wait," Lotty said.

Edmond ignored him.

"I trusted you! I gave you access to my home, and this is how you repay me?"

"Mr Edmond, please—"

"I don't want to hear it. Get out! All of you!"

Lotty raised both hands.

"Now, now. There's no need to get so upset."

"No need? You are trespassing!"

"Technically, we were invited."

"By her!"

"Yes. She's your employee, isn't she?"

"Not anymore!"

I was beginning to think that our investigation was going rather badly.

Then Lotty tilted his head, looked at Edmond for a moment, and said something I hadn't expected.

"You're so lonely, aren't you?"

Edmond stopped shouting and stared at him.

"What?"

"I can understand your feelings well.”

“You are too young to understand shit!"

"You have a lovely apartment, a nice little office, and a rather impressive plant. But you don't seem to have anyone to talk to."

"That's none of your business."

"Perhaps. But you've forbidden your housekeeper from entering one particular room, and you've made such a mystery out of it that she's started worrying about what might be happening inside. You must have known she'd become curious eventually."

Edmond's expression changed into something close to embarrassment.

Lotty glanced around the room, then back at him.

"Tell me, Edmond. How long have you been living alone?"

The old man didn't answer.

I looked at the fig palm by the window. It was a rather healthy-looking plant, considering the atmosphere in the apartment. For some reason, I had the feeling it was going to be more important than the bookshelf, the security camera, or anything else we'd found in that room.

But I couldn't have explained why.

Carol, meanwhile, looked as though she was waiting for the floor to open beneath her feet. She had been working for the old man for several months, and I imagine the prospect of losing a reasonably well-paid job in the middle of a pandemic wasn't appealing.

“I'm sorry, Mr Edmond,” she pleaded. “I shouldn't have brought them here without asking you. But nothing bad happened, right? I was just worried about you.”

“There's nothing to be worried about.”

“Then perhaps you could have told me that! There is nothing wrong here, why did you tell me not to enter? So many times, too!”

Edmond opened his mouth, but no immediate answer came out. Lotty, who had been observing this exchange with the expression of someone watching a particularly entertaining tennis match, finally decided to intervene.

“Edmond, may I ask you something?”

“No.”

“Are you always this welcoming to guests?”

“I don't invite guests.”

“That explains a great deal.”

I gave Lotty a look. We had already managed to get Carol into trouble, and I wasn't convinced that provoking her employer further was going to improve our situation. Lotty, however, had never been particularly concerned with making a good impression on people who weren't paying him.

“You seem to have misunderstood something,” he continued. “I don't think Carol came here because she wanted to snoop around your private office. She was worried about you.”

“I don't need anyone worrying about me.”

“Of course you don't. Nobody ever does. That's the problem, isn't it?”

Edmond narrowed his eyes.

Lotty gestured towards the room. “You forbid your housekeeper from entering, you occasionally disappear in here and talk to yourself, and you have enough security measures to make a small bank jealous. Naturally, she begins to wonder whether something is wrong. Perhaps you wanted her to become curious. Perhaps you wanted her to take an interest in you.”

“Don't be ridiculous.”

“It's a perfectly reasonable explanation. You live alone, you don't seem to have many visitors, and you've gone to considerable trouble to make one particular room mysterious. It might be your way of encouraging someone to pay attention.”

Edmond looked distinctly offended by the suggestion.

“I don't need attention.”

“Then I hope your intentions towards Carol are entirely paternal,” Lotty said. “Because I can send you to jail easily.”

Carol made a small choking noise. I wasn't sure whether she was trying not to laugh or trying to disappear. I—on the other hand—was in awe of the audacity. If Edmond called the police right now, the only people who are going to jail would be me and Lotty. 

“Of course I don’t harbour anything of the sort!” Edmond snapped. “I’m twice her age, that's disgusting!”

 “Good. Then we're all in agreement.”

Lotty smiled, demonstrating no intention of apologising for anything.

He reached into his coat and took out his phone.

“There's something else I'd like to ask you about.”

Edmond looked at the screen with immediate suspicion.

“I don't give out my personal information.”

“I haven't asked for it. I would, however, like you to look at a photograph.”

“Why?”

“Because I think you might recognise this gentleman.”

Lotty opened the picture Carol had shown us during our first meeting and held the phone out towards Edmond. Which… well, which meant that Lotty willingly spent some time on Tinder to look for Jake, for once. 

The old man glanced at it.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then his expression changed so abruptly that I almost wondered whether Lotty had shown him a photograph of Satan. The colour drained from Edmond's face, and his fingers tightened around the edge of the desk.

“Where did you get that?”

“From Carol.”

Edmond turned towards her.

“Why do you have a photograph of this man?”

“He's my former boyfriend,” Carol explained. “Well, we still meet occasionally, but we're not together anymore.”

“Why is he in your life?”

“Because we're friends?”

Edmond seemed to find this answer even more disturbing.

Lotty took the phone back. “You know him, then?”

“I didn't say that.”

“You certainly reacted as if you did. Does the name Jacob Brody sound familiar?”

Edmond stared at the photograph again, although Lotty had already lowered the phone.

“That man might be after something of mine,” he said quietly.

Lotty's expression became more attentive. “Something you own?”

Edmond nodded once.

“What exactly?”

“It's none of your business.”

“Perhaps not. But you have just suggested that a man connected to our client might be planning to steal from you. And I am a private detective, you know? I was born to handle those things.”

“I won't discuss it.”

Lotty waited. Edmond looked away, his jaw set, and I could see that we weren't going to get anything more out of him by asking the same question in a different tone.

“Does this young gentleman know where you live?” Lotty asked anyway. “Did he try to contact you recently?”

“Not your business.”

“Ah! So he did! You know you can pay me to take care of this business, right?”

Edmond's eyes moved towards the photograph on Lotty's phone.

“I don't want men in my business. I've told you that already. The last two housekeepers were men, and both of them were dishonest. One stole money, and the other went through my belongings. I won't make that mistake again.”

“Perhaps you've simply had bad luck.”

“Bad luck?” Edmond gave a short, unpleasant laugh. “You think dishonesty is a matter of luck? No, all honest men are dead, dead a long time ago. I will never trust one again.”

I was beginning to understand why his previous employees hadn't lasted long. It wasn't simply that Edmond was unpleasant. He seemed to be genuinely paranoid.

“Very well,” Lotty said. “You don't have to tell us what the object is. But if you believe this man may be trying to steal it, you can hire me to protect it. I will put the bad man in jail, I promise.”

“Why should I trust you?”

“You don't have to. You can pay me to do a job, and then decide whether I've done it properly.”

Surprisingly Edmond considered this.

“And what would you want in return?”

“I’ll work pro bono at first, until we confirm there really is a case to take care of. After that I got paid hourly, nothing unusual. But you must reconsider firing Carol. She did nothing wrong.”

The old man looked towards her.

“She brought strangers into my home.”

“She brought a detective into your home because she was worried about you. Besides, if you dismiss her now, you'll have to find another housekeeper, explain your requirements, and spend several weeks deciding that the new one is also untrustworthy. It sounds exhausting.”

I swear Lotty can sweetalk a rock into giving him a ride. The only thing I don’t get is why he was so invested in this bizarre case.

“Fine,” muttered Edmond. “She can stay. For now.”

Carol let out a breath she had been holding for the past ten minutes.

“Thank you, Mr Edmond.”

“Don't thank me. Just do your job.”

Lotty put his phone away. “Now, Carol, I need you to do something for me.”

She looked at him cautiously. “What?”

“I want you to contact Jake.”

“Why? Is he… a criminal?”

“We have no idea for now. Just tell him… tell him you've had enough of working for Edmond. Say that he's unbearable, that he's constantly suspicious of you, and that you've decided to quit.”

Carol frowned. “But I haven't decided to quit.”

“Precisely. That's why I want you to lie to him.”

She looked from Lotty to me, probably hoping I might offer a more sensible explanation. I couldn't. I had a vague suspicion that Lotty had just found a new direction, but I had no idea what it was supposed to achieve.

“Do you think he'll know something?” she asked.

“I think his reaction might be interesting.”

“Should I ask him anything?”

“No. Just tell him you're leaving. Don't make it sound like you're asking for advice, and don't mention us.”

Edmond was watching us with obvious distrust, but Carol still took out her phone.

“Why are you involving him?”

“Because we created a situation here,” Lotty replied. “And I would like to find out if your Jake would find it stimulating.”

“I don't want him anywhere near me,” interrupted Edmond.

“Then I suggest you let me handle it.”

There was something in Lotty's tone that made Edmond stop arguing. It was probably the same quality that made people hire him in the first place, despite his general appearance of having wandered into the wrong profession by accident.

Carol opened her messages and found Jake's conversation. She typed a short message, read it twice, and then looked at Lotty.

“Is this all right?”

Lotty glanced at the screen and nodded.

She pressed send.

Somewhere else in Bohemia, Jake Brody received a message from Carol informing him that she had finally had enough of her elderly employer and intended to quit.

And, judging by what happened next, he found it very stimulating.

***

I need to clarify that I didn't follow Carol the next day, of course. I had better things to do, such as ordering Christmas presents, printing out more Christmas cards, and wondering why Lotty had decided that an elderly man's secret was worth so much trouble. Besides, we already knew that something was going to happen. Lotty had made sure of that by asking Carol to send her former boyfriend a message about quitting her job.

What I didn't know at the time was that Jake had taken the message rather more seriously than we expected.

According to Carol, she was on her way to Edmond's apartment when a car drove through a puddle and splashed mud all over her clothes. Apparently the driver had managed to hit it at just the right angle to cover her from head to toe. She was understandably annoyed, especially since she was already running a little late. There was no point in arriving at Edmond's apartment looking like she'd just crawled out of a ditch, so she went back home to change.

It was an unfortunate accident. Or, at least, that's what she thought at the time.

While Carol was changing, another Carol arrived at Edmond's apartment, opening the door with her own key.

“She” was wearing a medical mask, had a similar build and height, and, as far as anyone could tell, looked enough like Carol to pass for her. Still, I imagine the “fake Carol” was quite happy that the old man wasn't home.

The impostor went straight to the forbidden room.

They didn't waste time looking at the desk or the bookshelves. Instead, they approached the fig palm, crouched beside the pot, and began digging through the soil.

That was when Lotty, Edmond, and I arrived.

I should probably explain that Lotty had arranged for us to be there at precisely that time. He had also arranged for Nikos to stay nearby, although I wasn't told exactly what his role would be. I had assumed we were going to have a conversation with Jake, perhaps followed by some rather uncomfortable questions. I hadn't expected to find someone halfway through redecorating Edmond's office with potting soil.

The moment we entered, the impostor looked up.

“What's going on?” the old man demanded, clearly furious.

Lotty on the other hand didn't seem surprised at all. He looked at the open pot, then at the person kneeling beside it.

“Mr Jake Brody, I suppose? I'm afraid we've caught you breaking into this apartment,” he said. “The police are already on their way.”

I glanced at him. I was fairly certain the police weren't on their way. Lotty was a big fat liar, and he was rarely so considerate as to involve actual police officers before the situation became interesting.

The impostor stood up, brushing soil from his hands.

“It's a joke,” he said immediately. “I'm just playing a prank on my girlfriend.”

“Is that so?”

“Yes. You can ask her.”

“I'd be delighted to. Unfortunately, your girlfriend isn't here, but I feel that you and the owner of this cute palm tree have a lot to say to each other.”

The man looked towards Edmond, who was standing behind us, staring at the mess around the fig palm.

“I don’t know him,” the impostor insisted. “Please call my girlfriend, Carol. She works here.”

Edmond looked very close to having an aneurysm from the shire hatred.

“Why are you digging in my plant then, ha? What are you looking to find there?”

The impostor hesitated. “I dropped something.”

“Into the pot?”

“It's a long story.”

“I imagine it is,” Lotty said. “And somewhere there is an explanation for why you stole the key from your girlfriend and made a copy.”

He walked past the impostor and crouched beside the pot. I expected him to examine the disturbed soil, perhaps look for whatever the man claimed to have dropped. Instead, he pushed his hand into the earth and began digging too.

“Don't touch that!” Edmond shouted.

Lotty ignored him.

“Stop!” Edmond moved towards him, suddenly much more energetic than I'd ever seen him. “You have no right to do that!”

“I'm afraid I need to know what’s inside.”

“That's my plant, don’t hurt it!”

“I'm as gentle as possible.”

“Just leave it alone!”

Lotty pulled something out of the soil. At the moment I — the only person who had no idea what was happening — thought his fingers were empty. Combining with the triumphant expression on his face the only possible conclusion was that Lotty cracked his pot at last. 

Now I know that there, under the thirty-years old fig tree, was buried a diamond.

It was large enough to cost billions of euros. It caught the light as Lotty twirled it between his fingers, and for a moment left me stunned.

I mean, a diamond! Under the fig tree! Have you ever seen that in your life? 

Edmond looked at it as though he'd just seen a ghost.

The impostor took a step closer. “That's not yours,” he said. “Give it to me, mister.”

Lotty raised an eyebrow. “Interesting. I don't remember asking you. I also very distinctly remember that any treasure found in Bohemian soil belongs to our country. And I found this little stone in the soil just now, what a coincidence!”

“You can't take it!”

“Even if you discard the Queen’s law, we are now in someone else’s apartment. ”

“You have no idea what’s going on here!”

In reality the only person in the room who had no idea was me. 

Edmond, who was really quiet all this time, made a sound somewhere between a gasp and a laugh. He looked from the diamond to the man, then back again.

“You are her son, aren’t you?”

The impostor didn't answer.

Lotty stood up, holding the diamond in one hand. “I think we really need to transfer this case to the local authorities.”

At that moment, the door was opened again and Nikos entered, wearing a police uniform.

I would like to say that he looked convincing. He did not. The uniform was probably the correct colour, and he had made an effort with the cap, but the fit was too tight. Plus there was his expression that suggested Nikos was enjoying himself far too much to be taken seriously as a representative of law.

Nevertheless, the effect on others was immediate.

“Police!” Nikos announced. “Stay where you are!”

The impostor turned towards him, looking genuinely frightened.

“Wait,” he said. “You can't arrest me!”

“I'm not sure anyone's mentioned arresting you yet,” Lotty replied.

“Listen to me, officer! I… this old man was mistreating my girlfriend and I needed to check on her! I have no idea why those people are talking about diamonds!”

“Your former girlfriend,” I corrected automatically. “Who now refers to you as a hookup.”

Nikos stepped closer, enjoying it tremendously. “You should come with me, sir. Are you a citizen of Bohemia? Do you have any documents that could confirm your identity and the present address?”

The man backed away until he hit the wall.

“What's this about? I haven't done anything!”

Lotty glanced at the open flowerpot.

“Of course not. You were simply visiting your girlfriend and decided to do a little gardening.”

The impostor looked at the door. Nikos moved to block it.

“Your name,” Lotty said, “is Jacob Brody, isn't it? But, if you are planning a crime, better not to tell people your real name, don’t you think? So you told Carol that your name is Jake, but didn’t even bother to charge a surname. How lazy is it? A person doesn’t need to be a genius to check the name on the mailbox and find out that this apartment belongs to Edmond Brody.”

I blinked. 

I never checked the name on the mailbox. And Carol didn’t either, it seems. Who does it in the 21st century?

And then my imagination went wild. The old man Edmond and the young man Jakob shared a surname, you see? They must be related then? And the broken "Are you her son?” that Edmond uttered before—all signs of the love story in the past! Did Enmond fall in love with his mom many years ago? Or, maybe even more dramatic—was Edmond his long lost dad?

The stories filled my head, each one of them more exciting than the other. 

And each of them completely wrong, of course. 

Lotty handed the diamond to Edmond, who accepted it with trembling fingers.

“Now,” he said solemnly, brushing dirt from his hands, “I think it's time we all get spanked with the paddle of truth.”

It didn’t dissolve the tension, but for a very brief moment made me wish that I never met Lotty. Then he continued talking:

“Your father’s name is Fox Brody,” he asked Jake. “And your mother is Margarita Brody, correct?”

“Yes, and what?”

“Don’t you think this nice diamond should belong to you?”

Jake glanced at the old man. “Okay, fair. I’ll tell you, because yes, you are right. It should be mine. My father told me about it, how they stole it together.”

Edmond paled. I could almost see the old memories returning, although I had no idea what they were. Until then, I'd assumed the diamond was simply something Edmond had acquired and hidden away for reasons of his own paranoia. Apparently, there was more to the story.

“Your father told you that?” Edmond asked.

“Yes, he did. He said it was his too, and that you had kept it all these years. He wanted me to get it back.”

The old man sat down heavily in the chair beside the desk.

And so the story came to light.

If you are still confused, let me summarise it for you, based on the things I found out later. I still have no idea how Lotty learned all this, and how much he knew on the day of the conflict. About half of it, most probably, and another half he pulled through lies and manipulation.

Thirty years ago young accountant Edmond and young tattoo artist Fox had been married. Yes, that’s the reason behind the same surname. They had also been very poor and their life was difficult, without any hope of getting better. Until a big mafia guy was suddenly shot in the tattoo shop where Fox was working at the time. Fox got daring and stole the ring from the dead body, first placing it under the rug—just in case other mafia guys would come looking for it later. Apparently it hadn't been registered or reported stolen in any official way, so after a few months of waiting Ed and Fox found themselves in the possession of a gemstone that potentially cost billions. 

However, it was easier in theory than in practice. How would an ordinary person sell the unregistered gemstone? Where to find a buyer? What if the buyer recognizes it as stolen from the mafia boss? The stone was right here, and they still had no idea how to exchange it for money, so the two men decided to hide it until they could find a suitable opportunity.

Their hiding place was the pot of a tiny fig palm. It was the only thing they owned together, and they both had been quite attached to it. The plant had survived the years, as had the diamond, although the relationship hadn't.

Fox eventually became involved with his female employer, Margarita. She became pregnant and offered him her business if he married her. Fox decided to accept. He explained the situation to Edmond and left him the diamond before going away to start a new life.

“Get lost,” said Edmond on that day, almost thirty years ago, “and if I ever see you again, I will crack your head.”

Still, Edmond never changed his name back after the divorce, and had kept the fig plant ever since.

Until now, when the grown up son of Fox and Margarita apparently decided to check if the diamond was still in the pot.

“So, your father told you to retrieve the stone?” Lotty asked. “When was it?”

Jake looked at him. “A few months ago.”

“And it was just a nice coincidence that your girlfriend is tall and usually wears a medical mask?”

“Listen, I know it looks bad, but I really like Carol. You can ask her! We are good friends.”

“Do you or your father have any recent debts?”

Jake hesitated.

“It is not relevant. What use is for the stone to stay in the pot, anyway? It officially belongs to no one! We can report it as a discovered treasure and get some money from the country, it’s absolutely legal. You said yourself, it’s legal. But Edmond didn’t even let me talk to him, and my father… he just wanted me to have the money.”

“How generous.”

“He said the diamond belonged to him as much as it belonged to Edmond. Even more so! He stole it first!”

“Did he?”

“Yes.”

“And he specifically asked you to steal it back?”

Jake nodded.

Edmond's expression had become even more distressed. He looked down at the diamond again, his fingers closing around it.

“After all these years,” he said. “He now wants to take it from me.”

Lotty tilted his head. “It seems so. Men are trash, aren’t they?”

“All honest men are dead,” Edmond muttered. “He who betrayed once will betray twice, there is no trust left in the world, only rotten traitors.”

I glanced at Lotty. He looked as though he agreed entirely.

“Unfortunately, men cannot be trusted,” he said with the feeling—a person who identified as a man last time I checked. 

Nikos gave him an alarmed look. “No, wait! You can't possibly be serious!”

“Darling, look around you. We have a man who stole from the dead, a man who lied to his girlfriend, impersonated her, and broke into someone's home. And a very handsome man who lies constantly,” he pointed at himself. “What else do you need?”

Nikos shook his head. 

“No, it can’t be right! I just feel it can’t be! I don’t believe his father actually sent him.”

“Why? You heard the story.”

“I heard half of the story. Can we ask Fox now?” Nikos flattered his lashes, already forgetting that he is supposed to impersonate a police force. “Pretty please?”

Lotty sighed. “My time is not unlimited. Plus, Fox could also lie. He could say—oh no, I never heard of this evil plot!—and what? You expect me to trust his words because they're less depressing?”

“I expect you to get to the bottom of it. Because you are smarter than all of us, babe!”

Flattering Lotty is usually pointless. He is so full of himself most of the time, that flattery does nothing. But Nikos looked too honest, too eager to defend all men from the unjust discrimination.

I, on the other hand, could see that Lotty didn’t finish his play. He had something else in his mind, and I couldn't understand what. 

Then Lotty spoke again.

“Tell me, Jake. Did your father also teach you to steal from your girlfriends?”

Jake's expression changed.

“What?”

“I've been looking into you. You have gambling debts, and a rather unpleasant history with several former girlfriends. Apparently, money and designer bags have a habit of disappearing whenever you're around.”

“That's not true! What… what are you talking about?!”

Lotty continued, almost conversationally. “I find it curious that you blame your father for this particular theft. You say he sent you, but I wonder whether he even knows you're here. Perhaps you simply remembered his story, decided you needed money, and thought it would be convenient to make him responsible for your actions.”

“My father told me to get the diamond!”

“Of course he did. And I suppose he also made you gamble, run up debts, and steal from women who trusted you. Poor thing. Your father must have been terribly neglectful. Didn't Daddy love you enough?”

“Shut up!”

I stared at Lotty.

It was not that I had never seen him be cruel. He could be remarkably unpleasant when he wanted to be, and he had an unfortunate tendency to discover people's insecurities and press on them until something broke. But this was different. And why? Even now, Jake seemed considerably more likable than Edmond, who strongly reminded Ebenezer Scrooge in every possible way. 

Perhaps it was because Lotty had recognised something in him that the rest of us hadn't.

I've often wondered whether Lotty can tell when people are lying because he has spent so much of his life doing it himself. He lies about his whereabouts, his intentions, his relationships, and occasionally even about whether he's eaten lunch. I wouldn't say he's particularly good at lying, but he does it often enough to recognise the technique in others.

Whatever the reason, he seemed quite certain that Jake wasn't telling the truth.

“Let's call the dad!” Nikos proposed. “Let’s just ask him if he knows where Jake is.”

Lotty gave him a long-suffering look, then took out his phone.

I have no idea where he got the number. Neither he was able to repeat the same trick again, when he lost his dentist’s number merely two weeks later. Which led to a very unpleasant trip through the countryside at 3AM, but that’s the story for another day.

Fox Brody answered after several rings. As if forgetting all previous accusations, Lotty honestly explained who he was, where we were, and why we were calling. Then he put the phone on speaker.

When Edmond heard Fox's voice, he stood up.

“Fox?”

There was a pause.

“Edmond?”

The old man gripped the desk.

“Did you send your son here to steal the diamond?”

“What? No!” Fox sounded genuinely confused. “It's still there?” he asked after a moment.

Edmond looked down at the diamond.

“Yes.”

Another pause, followed by rapid questioning:

“In the pot? Is the fig growing okay? I told you to sell the stone, why didn’t you?” 

“It’s still not possible, you fool.”

“I see.”

It wasn't much of a conversation, but it was considerably more than the two men had apparently managed in thirty years.

Jake tried to interrupt, insisting that his father was lying, but nobody seemed particularly interested in listening anymore. Lotty ended the call and told Nikos to contact the real police. Nikos finally removed his rather unconvincing policeman's cap, looking relieved that he would soon be able to return to being a civilian.

Jake was taken away a little later. He continued protesting his innocence, although he had become noticeably less confident once the police started asking about his previous financial troubles.

Edmond remained behind, looking somewhere between relieved and stunned.

“Fox didn't send him,” he said quietly.

“No,” Lotty replied. “It seems he didn't. Should we call Carol? She can talk you through it.”

Edmond looked towards the fig palm. Its soil was scattered across the floor, and one of its lower branches had been bent during the search. He reached out and carefully straightened it.

“Thirty years,” he murmured.

I wasn't sure whether he meant the diamond, the plant, or Fox.

Perhaps all three.

And that is how the case of the missing diamond was solved. Or, more accurately, how a diamond that nobody had reported missing was found in a flowerpot, recovered from an attempted theft, and returned to the Queen and country after thirty years.

I suppose I should have written this story down sooner. However, at the time, I had other things to do, and, frankly, the whole affair seemed rather too ridiculous to deserve a proper account. It was only when Lotty mentioned it again recently that I realised how much time had passed.

COVID is over now. People have stopped arguing about whether they should wear masks, although they've found plenty of other things to argue about. The world has returned to its usual level of chaos, with new diseases, new political scandals, and new reasons to avoid crowded places. Some things never change.

But other things do. 

Carol graduated from medical school and became a doctor, as she'd always intended. She still has strong opinions about public health, and she remains a particularly enthusiastic supporter of wearing masks on public transport. In fact, she campaigned for it so persistently that Bohemia eventually introduced free masks at train stations and bus terminals. I don't know whether this was entirely her achievement, but she certainly likes to take credit for it.

The diamond was returned to Bohemia, and Carol received the standard seven-percent treasure-finder reward. It was a rather substantial sum, enough to help her through the last years of school. Jake, meanwhile, went to prison for various thefts all his ex-girlfriends reported. I haven't heard from him since, which is probably for the best.

The more surprising development was what happened between Edmond and Fox.

After the incident, the two men began speaking again. At first, their conversations were rather awkward, I imagine, mostly consisting of cautious questions and long pauses. They eventually consulted lawyers to sort out the diamond mess for Carol. And somewhere along the way, Edmond and Fox decided that thirty years apart was quite enough and got married again.

I wasn't present for the proposal, and I don't know who asked whom. Given their history, I suspect the question was probably preceded by an argument about something entirely unrelated. But they did get married, and, as far as I know, they've been happy ever since.

Their Christmas gathering became something of a tradition. Every year, they invited the same small group of people who had somehow become involved in their story: Carol, Lotty, Nikos, and me.

This year was no exception.

By the time we arrived, Fox was in the kitchen, Edmond was complaining about the temperature, and Carol was helping set the table. The fig palm stood in the corner of the living room, looking perfectly healthy and considerably less suspicious than it had during our first visit. I noticed that the pot had been replaced with a much sturdier one.

“Afraid someone might dig it up again?” I asked Edmond.

He gave me a stern look.

“It's a good plant. I don't want it damaged.”

“Of course.”

Fox appeared behind him carrying a tray of drinks.

“Don't tease him. He's been very protective of that plant lately.”

“I've always been protective of it,” Edmond said.

“Yes, darling. I know.”

Fox kissed his cheek, and Edmond pretended to be annoyed. It was rather sweet, although I would never tell either of them so.

Carol was telling Lotty about her hospital when we came into the dining room. She had been working long shifts, and Lotty was listening with the expression of someone who had just discovered that other people had jobs.

“It's a demanding profession,” he said. “I can’t imagine how hard it is for you, dear.”

“Yes,” Carol replied. “Some days I barely have time to sit down.”

“You can still become a detective. We spend a great deal of time sitting down.”

I looked at him.

“Speak for yourself.”

“I'm speaking for both of us.”

Nikos arrived a little later, carrying a beautifully wrapped present. He had become quite successful as a stripper, which was probably the most sensible career choice anyone in our group had ever made. He was no longer Lotty's boyfriend, although their separation had done very little to change the way they behaved around each other.

In fact, Lotty had recently become single again.

I mention this because Nikos was also single, and because the two of them had spent most of the evening exchanging the sort of looks that usually precede either a romantic reunion or at least particularly intense one nighter.

“Your tie is bonkers,” Nikos told Lotty as he passed him.

“Thanks, honey, it’s a gift from the prime-minister.”

“It's crooked, though.”

“Perhaps it lacks just a little nudge in the right direction.”

Nikos smiled. “I am very good at nudges if you catch my meaning. Also nudges without ‘g’, it’s literally my job.”

Lotty looked at him for a moment, then adjusted his tie.

I decided not to comment. There are some things in life one learns to leave alone, and Lotty's romantic arrangements are definitely among them.

Dinner was still pleasant. Fox had prepared far too much food, Edmond complained that nobody was eating enough, and Carol told us about a patient who had mistaken a thermometer for a rather different medical instrument. Nikos shared stories from his latest field job, most of which involved men jumping out of cakes, and Lotty offered unsolicited advice on how to improve the cakes for the future events.

At some point, Fox raised his glass.

“To old friends,” he said.

Edmond looked at him, then raised his own.

“To second chances.”

It was a nice moment. Nobody made a joke, nobody interrupted, and even Lotty managed to remain quiet for several seconds.

Then Nikos leaned towards him and whispered something in his ear.

Lotty smiled.

I don't know what he said, but I suspect it had something to do with nudes.

After all, Nikos was very good at his job, and Lotty had never been particularly good at staying single.

As for me, I had already decided that if they got married, I would refuse to wear pink.

But I will still be the one to photograph the ceremony, because that’s what I’m good at, right?
